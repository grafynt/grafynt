## The short version

- Build a fixed list of buying-intent questions in your buyers' own wording, not your service names.
- Run them across several assistants, logged out, with the right location set, and record which businesses are named.
- Run the same questions in ordinary search and note where the two disagree.
- Verify a crawler can actually fetch and read your key pages.
- Sweep your site, Google Business Profile and listings for wording that disagrees with itself.
- Keep the question set fixed so the next run is comparable. A baseline you cannot repeat is not a measurement.

## Step one: write the question list

This is the step that decides whether the audit is worth anything, and the one most people get wrong by using their own vocabulary.

You need the questions a buyer asks in the last few minutes before choosing. Not "aircon services Jakarta" but "who can fix an aircon today in South Jakarta", "is it cheaper to repair or replace an old aircon", "which aircon service is open on Sunday". Include comparisons, because comparison questions are where assistants most often name a specific set of businesses. Include price questions, which are the ones buyers ask and businesses most avoid answering.

Ten to twenty questions is enough. Write them down and do not change them, because the point is to re-run exactly these later.

## Step two: run the prompt panel

Ask each question in each assistant. ChatGPT, Gemini, Perplexity and Google AI Overviews is a reasonable panel; add whatever your market uses.

Three rules make the results mean something. Log out, or use a private window, so your own history does not bias the answer toward you. Set the location you actually sell into, not the one you happen to be sitting in. Ask each question in each engine more than once, because retrieval is not deterministic and a single run is anecdote.

For each question, record: were you named, were you named accurately, and who else was named. That third column is the valuable one and the one people forget to write down. The named competitors are the actual competitive set as the engines see it, which is frequently not the set the owner would have listed.

## Step three: check retrieval

An invisible page explains most bad results, so verify it rather than assuming.

Confirm your key pages return quickly to a plain request, and that the content you care about is present without JavaScript running. Check that prices, service lists and hours are real text and not baked into an image. Confirm robots rules are not blocking more than intended. Check that each service has its own URL, because a single-page site gives retrieval nothing specific to return.

## Step four: the consistency sweep

Put your website, your Google Business Profile and every third-party listing side by side and compare them field by field: business name, address format, hours, service areas, categories, and the exact wording of every service.

Every disagreement you find is a reason an assistant cannot state a fact about you confidently. This is the least interesting step and it is usually where the problem is.

## What a finished audit looks like

| Output | What it tells you |
| --- | --- |
| Question list with named businesses | Whether you are in the conversation at all |
| Accuracy notes per mention | Whether being named is helping or hurting |
| Competitor set, by name | Who is currently capturing your demand |
| Search vs assistant comparison | Whether this is a ranking problem or a citation problem |
| Retrieval check | Whether anything else on the list can even work |
| Consistency table | The cheapest fixes, usually the largest |
| A fixed baseline | The thing every later run is measured against |

## When this is a bad idea to do

Do not audit once and treat it as a result. A single run across a handful of questions is noise. Without a fixed question set and a repeat, you cannot tell improvement from randomness, and you will attribute both to whatever you changed.

Do not audit while logged into accounts the assistants have memory of. Personalisation will show you a flattering picture of your own visibility, which is worse than no information.

Do not audit before you can act on it. If you have no capacity to fix a retrieval problem or reconcile your listings in the next few months, the findings go stale and you have bought a document.

Do not expand the panel to every engine and phrasing you can think of. A huge unrepeatable sweep is less useful than twenty questions you will genuinely re-run next month.
