## What "order processing" actually covers

Most businesses mean four separate jobs when they say order processing, and they cost different amounts of attention to automate.

**Intake.** A customer sends an order by WhatsApp, a form, a marketplace, or a phone call someone types up afterwards. Automating intake means the details land in one structured place without anyone retyping them. This is where most of the time is lost and most of the errors are made.

**The record.** Every order becomes a row with the same fields — customer, items, total, payment status, delivery method. If your orders currently live across chat threads and someone's memory, this is the step that pays for itself first.

**Status back to the customer.** Confirmed, packed, shipped, out for delivery. Each of those is a message you are currently sending by hand or, more likely, not sending until the customer asks.

**Internal alerts.** Stock below a level, a payment not received after a set time, an order sitting unpacked. These are the ones people skip and then discover at the end of the month.

A first build usually does intake, the record and customer status. Alerts are cheap to add once the record exists, so they tend to come second.

## Build it or buy an off-the-shelf tool

| | Custom build | Off-the-shelf order tool |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cost shape | One-time, Rp 5.300.000 | Monthly, forever, per user |
| Fit | Matches how you already work | You change how you work to match it |
| Odd rules | Handled — deposits, split payments, resellers | Handled if the vendor thought of them |
| If you stop paying | It keeps running | It stops |
| Support | You own the workflow and can hand it to anyone | Vendor's queue |

Off-the-shelf wins when your process is genuinely standard and you would rather not own anything. A custom build wins when your ordering has quirks that a product would force you to abandon, or when a recurring per-seat fee over a few years costs more than owning the thing outright.

## When this is a bad idea

Do not automate order processing while the process itself is still being argued about. If two people in your business would describe the steps differently, automation will freeze whichever version happened to get written down. Agree the process on paper first — that part is free.

Do not do this if your order volume is low and irregular. Automation converts repeated manual minutes into a one-time cost. Without the repetition there is nothing to convert, and Rp 5.300.000 buys you a slightly faster version of something you barely do.

Do not do this if your orders are genuinely bespoke, where every one is negotiated on price, spec and timing. Those flows need a person and a good record of what was agreed, not an automated path. The record is worth building; the automation around it is not.

Do not do this if the real problem is that not enough orders arrive. A faster pipe does not fill an empty one. Start with the audit and find out which problem you have before spending anything.
