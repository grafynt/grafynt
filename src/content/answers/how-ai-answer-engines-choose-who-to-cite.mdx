## The short version

- Assistants mostly do not answer from memory. They run a search, read what comes back, and write a reply from it. That mechanism is retrieval-augmented generation, and it is why this is a solvable problem rather than a mysterious one.
- Three things decide whether you are the source quoted: whether your page can be retrieved, whether it answers the question outright, and whether everything else written about you agrees with it.
- Directness beats volume. One page that answers one question completely outperforms a long page that covers six topics adequately.
- Corroboration beats assertion. A claim repeated identically across your site, your Google Business Profile and your directory listings is treated very differently from the same claim made once.
- Being invisible is rarely a content shortage. It is usually inconsistency, or a page a crawler cannot read.

## How the mechanism actually works

When you ask an assistant for a supplier, a clinic or a contractor, it does not consult a stored list of businesses. It issues one or more search queries through a live web-search API, receives a set of pages, reads them, and composes an answer from what it found. Some of those pages get named. Most do not.

That has three consequences worth internalising.

**Your current pages are the input.** Not your reputation, not how long you have traded, not what you said last year. The pages that can be fetched right now are the whole evidence base for whatever the assistant says about you.

**The query is not your keyword.** The assistant rewrites a human request into one or more searches of its own. Someone typing "cheapest place near me to fix a cracked screen fast" may generate a search for a service phrase you have never put on your site. The questions worth targeting are the ones buyers ask, not the ones you would use to describe yourself.

**Only quotable text gets quoted.** The assistant needs a passage it can compress into a sentence without risking being wrong. A paragraph that answers a question completely, on its own, with no surrounding context required, is the ideal unit. A page that sets up context for three paragraphs before getting to the point supplies nothing liftable.

## The three things that decide it

**Retrievability.** If a crawler cannot read the page, nothing else matters. Content that only appears after JavaScript runs, content behind an interaction, and pages excluded by robots rules are all invisible to a surprising amount of retrieval. The same goes for a page that takes too long to return. This is unglamorous and it is the first thing to check.

**Directness.** The page has to answer the question asked, early, in plain words. Not approach the question. Not provide background first. One question per page, answered in the first paragraph, then supported. This is the whole reason the pages on this site are shaped the way they are.

**Corroboration.** This is the one most businesses get wrong without knowing. An assistant weighing whether to name you is implicitly asking whether the evidence agrees with itself. If your website calls a service one thing, your Google Business Profile calls it another, and a directory listing calls it a third, there is no consistent fact to state. A competitor whose three surfaces say the same thing in the same words is a safer source, so it gets named instead.

That is why keyword consistency is not a tidiness exercise. One service, one phrase, everywhere. It is the cheapest visibility work available to most businesses and almost nobody does it.

## What this means in practice

| Instinct | What actually earns the citation |
| --- | --- |
| Write more pages | Write one page per question buyers actually ask |
| Open with positioning | Answer in the first paragraph, support it afterwards |
| Vary the wording so it reads naturally | Use one fixed phrase per service, everywhere |
| Add schema describing the business | Add schema describing text visible on the page |
| Make the site visually impressive | Make it readable with JavaScript disabled |
| Target high-volume keywords | Target buying-intent questions, however narrow |
| Chase the biggest competitor | Find the questions nobody in your market has answered |

## When this is a bad idea to chase

Do not chase citation for questions that do not precede a purchase. Being named as a source on a general-interest topic is pleasant and sells nothing. The questions worth owning are the ones someone asks in the last few minutes before they choose a supplier.

Do not optimise for one assistant. They differ in how they retrieve, and tuning to the quirks of whichever one you personally use is how you end up with a page that works in one place and nowhere else. Answer the question properly and you are legible to all of them.

Do not add schema to describe text you are not willing to show a reader. Markup describing hidden content is a manual-action risk, not a shortcut, and it is a bad trade for a short-term gain.

Do not start here if nobody can find you in ordinary search either. Classic indexing and AI citation share most of their foundations. Fix the foundations once and both improve; chase the newer acronym alone and you will pay twice.
